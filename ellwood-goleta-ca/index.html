Where Ellwood sits

The City of Goleta takes in the Ellwood Mesa open space, and a 2004 city approval placed a residential subdivision on the northwest corner of Santa Barbara Shores Park, south of Hollister Avenue between Pebble Beach Drive and Las Armas Road. No official boundary for Ellwood as a neighborhood is published, so the location here is approximate.

The city notice describes that approval as an 84-lot subdivision on 36 acres, with 78 lots for single-family houses. We did not confirm how many of those homes stand today, and we found no survey of building age or construction in Ellwood. This page therefore makes no claims about when homes were built or what they contain.

How the open space shaped the area

The history matters for one reason. Ellwood Mesa was once slated for 161 homes, and after years of dispute the City of Goleta took ownership of the mesa on February 4, 2005, according to the city. In exchange a 36-acre part of Santa Barbara Shores Park was approved for homes, and the mesa stayed public open space.

So the homes near Pebble Beach Drive sit beside protected open ground and a coastal bluff edge. That is a fact about where people live, not a record of floods. We found no documented flood or storm damage tied to Ellwood, and neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status is not verified.

Regional storms are not Ellwood records

The National Weather Service issued a flash flood warning for the Santa Barbara South Coast on January 3, 2026, and Noozhawk reported more than 1.25 inches of rain in an hour in Goleta. NASA Earth Observatory recorded over 16 inches in two days in parts of Santa Barbara and Ventura counties in January 2023.

None of those reports names Ellwood, so we do not claim the area was damaged. They show how fast rain can arrive on this coast, which matters for roofs, windows and gutters on any house.

First-hour safety steps for an Ellwood home

  1. Keep people and pets out of any area where water touches outlets, cords or appliances.
  2. Close the fixture valve or the main water valve if you can reach it safely.
  3. Do not touch a breaker while standing in water. Switch off a circuit only from dry floor.
  4. Lift rugs, boxes and electronics off wet floors where it is safe.
  5. Photograph the source and the damage before moving anything.

Water heaters and power

A leaking water heater can sit near electrical connections and a gas line. If you smell gas, leave the house and call the gas utility from outside. California Health and Safety Code section 19211 requires water heaters to be braced against earthquake movement, so ask a plumber to check yours.

How extraction and drying work

A restoration crew pumps out standing water, then maps moisture in floors, walls and cabinets with meters and a thermal camera. Air movers and dehumidifiers run with repeat readings until materials reach a dry reference. Wet insulation and pad that cannot dry in place are removed, and hard flooring is checked before anyone decides whether it can stay.

Industry practice is set out in ANSI/IICRC S500, a published standard for water damage restoration. The EPA advises drying wet materials within 24 to 48 hours. See the water damage cleanup page, the water heater leak page and the roof leak page for the cause you are dealing with.

Insurance documentation

Coverage depends on the cause and the terms of your policy, so ask your insurer what applies. A sudden pipe failure is treated differently from slow seepage, and the California Department of Insurance says standard homeowners policies exclude flood. Photos, moisture readings and a written scope can help describe the damage, and none of them decides whether a claim is paid.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Leaving wet carpet or insulation in place to see if it dries.
  • Running a household vacuum on water that may be contaminated.
  • Painting over a stain before the source is found.
  • Assuming open space nearby means flood risk, or that it means none.
  • Discarding damaged items before photographing them.
